Power and Innovation

How to push for change when you're not in charge

While working for a large, enterprise tech company for the last ten years, I have heard the constant refrain from leadership for our teams to be change agents and to innovate! innovate! innovate! But what those in power say they want with their words and what they show they want with their actions are vastly different. The gap between rhetoric and resources is where innovation can be lost. Leaders say they want change (and may even believe they want change), but what I’ve often experienced is they are not willing to dedicate the time and resources required to bring real and lasting transformation. Even when I was on a team called the “Jumpstart Innovation Team” the executives in charge of this special project were hesitant to truly empower our team to deliver the change we were tasked to bring.

Executives want the impact of innovation without the disruption of transformation.

Which leaves us with some obvious and important questions: Why don’t more organizations demonstrate innovative transformation more regularly? What keeps those in power from truly wanting change? How can organizations change and innovate when those in charge are resistant?

MAINTAINING STATUS QUO

At the core of this issue is the desire to maintain power and control. This isn’t because those in leadership are power-hungry monsters. It’s a natural response.. When you’re winning, you don’t want to change your strategy. If you’re in power, your goal is to stay in power. There’s little upside to pushing for change when things are going your way. Those with power and control are very change averse because it adds what they perceive to be unnecessary risk. The status quo is in their favor so the belief is that change puts that in jeopardy. They prefer the devil they know to the devil they don’t. It’s the old “if it ain’t broke don’t fix it” mindset. The irony is that by avoiding the broken status quo, leaders often invite the very disastrous change they hope to prevent.

But a leader that prides themselves and their product or company on being transformative needs to give the appearance of continuously innovating. It’s a delicate dance for those in control to act like things are changing, push for improvement, and call for innovation while simultaneously maintaining the status quo, protecting their power, and minimizing risk and instability.

THE ONLY CONSTANT IS CHANGE

Resisting change is ultimately a futile and foolish decision. I’ve always loved building sandcastles at the beach. The best sandcastles have moats which means building perilously close to the water. My construction is always done to protect the towers from the tides with walls of sand and drainage ditches to divert excess water away from the city. But no matter how much I try, the tides will always win. At a much larger scale many ocean towns are fighting the same fight. On the North Carolina coast I’ve seen beach rejuvenation projects trying to fight off the inevitability of change with front-end loaders and giant pump systems sucking sand from deep in the ocean back to the shore. All this effort is only delaying the inevitable. You can’t fight off the ocean. You can’t stop the erosion. You can’t stop change.

While the ocean represents the external, unstoppable forces of the market, the slow wear of a car tire represents the internal decay of an organization that refuses to change. If you refuse to face the reality of change and never change the tires on your car, the tires will still change. Over time the tires will wear down, slowly changing despite your wish for things to remain the same. Over time that slow change will escalate to a big, disastrous change like a flat tire or a blowout while driving down the interstate.

Change is inevitable. You can try to ignore it. You can try to stop it. Or you can accept it and do what you can to shape the change into something productive rather than destructive. When your leaders appear to be ignoring or resisting change, you can help shift their mindset by pointing out the change that is happening regardless of their desires; changes in the market, changes in technology, changes in employee sentiment and skillsets, etc. Only when the inevitability of change is recognized will people begin to take control of how that change will happen.

STRATEGY VERSUS TACTIC

This blog isn’t aimed at executives to wag a finger at executives and leaders and say, “You should embrace real change.” Instead it is targeted at those of you who are pushing for change in organizations that are resistant. So I want to share a few approaches to transform a company or team’s culture from wherever you are in the organization.

To do this I want to explain some of the differences between strategy and tactic. Both are approaches to getting what you want. Strategy is the approach taken by those with power, control, resources, and time on their side. They have the luxury of sitting back and thinking about how to use what they have to achieve their goal and then they deploy those resources to achieve their desired outcome.

When people don’t have the upper hand when it comes to resources like money, power, or time they resort to tactics to get to their goals. Where strategy is proactive, tactics are reactive. It is a response to those with the power. Tactics are the tools used to maneuver within the existing power structure.

If you see a need for change in your organization but those setting the strategy only want to maintain the status quo, you need to rely on tactics to push for change.

First, consider your relationship to those that are in power. Is there psychological safety to be creative and to speak truth to power? In many organizations that are change resistant there is a corresponding lack of psychological safety. There is not a safe space to challenge leadership or take risks. So if there isn’t already that relationship, here are a few tactical approaches to help take small steps toward the change your organization needs.

Humor disarms. If you are able to speak directly to the decision makers, be personable, be lighthearted, have a sense of humor in your conversation. This will help build a trusting relationship with your leaders and keep them from taking a defensive posture when you bring up the subject of change. No one wants to hear that their approach isn’t the right approach.

Another skill to hone when pushing for change is story telling. Much like humor, stories entertain and draw people out of their defensive mindset. They can get people’s attention on an emotional level. Stories can also awaken imagination in ways that pure data can’t.

When trying to change someone’s mind on something, it’s often best to not attack the subject directly. Changing someone’s mind about a specific topic is more effective if you act like Bob Ross. People’s opinions are not isolated. They sit in the midst of their entire worldview. Sometimes you need to repaint the background of their understanding until what’s in the foreground doesn’t make sense any more. Give context and examples of what you notice that the lack of change might be causing without talking about the change at first. In practical terms, this means instead of asking for a change, consistently highlight the specific inefficiencies or missed opportunities the current direction creates until the “background” of the problem is so vivid that your solution becomes the only logical “foreground” choice.

Once you’ve created an environment more open to considering change, there are three key points you’ll want to focus on. First, highlight the impact versus the risk. Second, the change you’re calling for might be disruptive but it doesn’t have to be destructive.Finally, weigh the important versus the urgent.

TACTIC #1: WEIGH IMPACT VERSUS RISK

When I was first tasked with leading a new innovation team, my scrappy group of designers were excited to transform the business and introduce new and exciting products to the market. But our hopes were quickly tamped down by the reality of what our executives wanted. They didn’t want us to jump off cliffs or take wild moon shots. What they really wanted was risk mitigation. They wanted us to test innovative ideas before millions of dollars and many months were wasted chasing the wrong kind of change.

This approach was less glamorous, but much more fruitful for the business. The majority of our three month incubator projects produced moderate successes that could be counted as wins because our light-weight team was cheap and moved quickly so there was minimal risk relative to the impact we provided. But there were two projects that stuck out as major wins in my mind, although to most people only one of them would be seen as a big win.

The obvious big win was the initial incubation of what would become watsonx Orchestrate that now makes roughly $100 million per quarter. Our team put together the demo for the mainstage at THINK presented by our CEO. We took this project from R&D concept to mainstage demo in just three months with a small team of 5 designers. This was a tactical masterpiece. By using limited resources and a tight timeline, we provided the impact leadership wanted while minimizing the risk of a larger, slower rollout.

The other project I see as a big win you’ve never heard of because it never saw the light of day. One of our executives saw two products in our portfolio that were both doing observability and he wanted to know if there was any potential for integration between them. Our team quickly developed a few potential integration possibilities in consultation with end users of each tool, technical SMEs, and sales people. We then tested those concepts with real users. After just a few sprints it became clear that investing in a technical integration would be too expensive, have too little upside, and simply be too risky to pursue. Our recommendation was instead to start with a co-selling story. Years later one of the project managers told me that this “stop the bus” outcome saved the company countless time and money building out the wrong solution.

For leaders to trust your change initiative, they need to trust that you are weighing impact against risk and are willing to stop the bus if the math doesn’t add up.

TACTIC #2: AIM FOR DISRUPTION, NOT DESTRUCTION

Change, by its nature, is disruptive. When leadership hears about change, the instinctive response is to think about what could be lost. A lot of a leader’s time is spent looking for and mitigating risk. When the word “change” comes up, the leader’s alarm bells go off. But disruption isn’t the same as destruction. Disruption differs from destruction in two ways.

First, the degree of impact is less in disruption. You can cast a vision for a robust transformation, but make sure you also have a tactical plan for incremental change. A series of small changes over time is a lot easier to accept than a full overhaul of the status quo. Make sure this is communicated clearly to the decision makers early. It’s a tricky balance because you want to excite them about the future but without scaring them away by what it will take to get there. Using phases like “imagine if…” and “I wonder what things would look like for us if…” softens the tangible and irreversible feeling of a new direction. It paints the picture but leaves it in a realm of hypothetical rather than actual. Then you can quickly follow that broad by ephemeral vision with some smaller steps toward that vision that are easier to swallow.

Next, the transformation is not irreversible when done in a disruptive rather than destructive way. Too often change is treated as if there’s no turning back. There is an assumption that to adopt a transformative change program, it’s all or nothing. But change can be done as exploration or experimentation. Teams can test the waters before fully committing. Our design organization talks about failing fast and failing cheaply. This means running research, building prototypes, and running trials on a smaller group before pushing in all the chips. Your first ask to leadership shouldn’t be permission to overhaul the entire business in permanent ways, but to take a small part of the business and run an experiment to test a hypothesis. This will further help soothe the fear of risk.

TACTIC #3: PRIORITIZE THE IMPORTANT OVER THE URGENT

Once you get even a little buy-in to your change proposal, you need to make sure to highlight the potential upside of the transformative change compared to the incremental impact of the current backlog. Change takes time. But the giant machines that exist have to continue producing what they were designed to produce. To bring real change, the urgent need to keep producing must be disrupted or at least deprioritized to some degree in order to bring about the long term, important change that leads to innovative differentiation.

If you can get the leadership of your team fired up for this change effort, it will be your duty to continue to stoke that fire or else it will cool with time and the attention will drift back to the small, short-term deliverables that support the status quo. Teams cannot do everything. Priorities have to be set. Resources must be allocated. The cost of bringing about the change shouldn’t necessarily bankrupt the existing work in progress and backlog, but if leaders truly believe in the vision you’re selling them, there will need to be tradeoffs. Work with your leaders to redefine priorities based on what’s important and not just what’s urgent. This will require helping them to look further down the road to see the true impact of what is prioritized. We might make a small impact with an urgent work item this sprint, but if we invest in the change initiative instead over the next three sprints, we will see exponential value add. Use data. Use numbers. Show the larger scope of what’s important and put what people think is urgent today into perspective. Is it really that urgent? What happens if we delay or cancel that work item that seems so urgent today in favor of something we know will be important tomorrow?

These conversations and this kind of perspective change can’t happen in one meeting. You need to create and maintain an ongoing relationship and conversation with your leaders and keep coming back to these three concepts: impact versus risk, disruptive not destructive, and important over urgent.

LEADERS WHO SEEK ACCOUNTABILITY

However, there are times when those in control or those in leadership of organizations with power task others with the job of challenging their own current thoughts and actions. A classic example of this is the advisor to a monarch. The monarch asks this trusted advisor to speak the truth even when it goes against the ruler’s perspectives, decisions, or actions. Those who have been tasked with this will find themselves in a tricky situation. Speaking truth to power is never easy.

Even when a leader might think they want to hear about ways to change, actually receiving this kind of feedback can lead to defensiveness. The leaders are saying they don’t know what they don’t know and they need someone else to identify that for them. But when they come face to face with a hard truth, they often bristle. They were expecting something smaller, a more manageable problem, a less risky direction, or a truth that reflected less poorly on their own character or capability. They want something simple or a quick fix and are left with having to decide if the effort, resources, and risk are worth the value added by addressing something they didn’t even know was an issue moments before.

So what can someone do when there are people in positions of power over them who are resistant or outright against facing reality and accepting necessary change into their organization? Humor and storytelling often are used to ease leaders into the story, but stories and metaphors can also be used to shock leaders into paying attention.

We can learn a few lessons from St. Basil. Ivan the Terrible was a powerful leader whose reputation is captured in his title. Yet St. Basil somehow found a way to offer reproach to Ivan the Terrible, scold him publicly, and call for drastic changes without dire consequences. One time, Ivan was fasting from eating meat for Lent right after instigating a horrible massacre. Basil walked in and dropped a slab of raw meat and cups of blood on the table in front of the tzar and said, “That is your food and drink”. When the tzar refused to eat it on the grounds of being a devout Orthodox Christian, Basil questioned how Ivan could follow the traditions of Lent while simultaneously killing Christians. He told Ivan to stop the murder. In response, Ivan did not punish or kill Basil as everyone expected, but instead sent him gifts and promised to keep him safe. This was not an isolated incident, but the normal way that Basil would interact with a powerful and vengeful lead. And yet, after Basil died Ivan the Terrible respected St. Basil so much that he served as a pall bearer at Basil’s funeral.

How did Basil get away with it? He used the tactics of the “holy fool”. By dramatizing his message through bold, symbolic actions he got the tzar’s attention, evoked strong emotion, and punctuated the scene with a clear, fact based truth that contracted the tzar’s current way of thinking and acting. But even if the ideal environment isn’t created for truth tellers there are tactics that status quo questioners can use to aid in their quest to push things forward.

TAKE THE FIRST STEP

At the end of the day, change can only happen if someone steps up, takes a risk, and acts as the catalyst for the change. Leaders don’t want to risk their career chasing unlikely dreams. Likewise, if you are honest with yourself, you are also probably a bit hesitant to stick your neck out and demand change. It’s easy to scapegoat our leaders and say they aren’t willing to take a risk on a big bet. But look in the mirror and ask yourself if you’re willing to push for something truly innovative and take the risk of failure on your shoulders. If you’ve read this far, I believe you do have that passion and courage. Strategy may belong to the powerful, but the future can be steered by the tacticians. Stop waiting for permission to innovate. Pick up your brush, find your “holy fool” moment, and start shaping the change that is already coming.
